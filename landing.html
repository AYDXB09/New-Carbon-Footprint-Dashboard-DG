Breadth × depth

One grade for the whole building is the wrong shape for a school.

The facilities office has twelve months of half-hourly meter data. Nobody has ever surveyed how the Year 8s get to school. Those two facts should not have to average into a single credibility score — so here they don't.

How it usually goes

A single rigour setting

Pick "simple" and your good energy data is thrown away. Pick "comprehensive" and you're blocked at the first field you can't fill — usually a commuting question no school has ever been asked before.

The output is one number with no stated error, which is exactly the number a board member will ask you to defend.

How Groundwork goes

A tier per category, changeable any week

Upload the electricity bills today and energy jumps to Tier 2 on its own. Run a mode-share survey in March and transportation moves up while nothing else is touched.

Every category carries its tier as a visible badge, and the confidence range around its number widens or narrows to match. Improving your data is something you can literally watch happen.

The number, and its edges

Every figure arrives as a range, because every input is one.

Emission factors are published as intervals, not constants. Groundwork simulates across those intervals thousands of times and reports the spread that comes back. A Tier 1 category produces a wide band automatically; a Tier 3 one produces a narrow band automatically. There is no separate confidence score to argue about — the width is the score.

Where the uncertainty actually lives

Transport is 27% of this school's footprint and carries almost all of its error. Running one mode-share survey would cut the whole-school range by roughly a third — worth more than another year of half-hourly meter data.

Groundwork ranks that for you, so the next thing you spend a term on is the thing that moves the number.

Peer standing

Locked. Percentile ranking needs a real cohort of comparable schools — same setting, same climate region, same data tier — and that cohort doesn't exist yet. You'll see a bar here when it does, and not before.

How a number gets made

Four steps, each one auditable a year later.

Nothing is derived from a source we can't point at, and nothing enters your file without a human agreeing to it first.

01

Give the period, not the year

Every row carries an explicit start and end date. An August-to-July academic year and a January-to-December calendar year are both twelve months and both usable; a five-week snapshot is labelled a spot check and never annualised by naive multiplication.

Partial years are extended using a seasonal shape derived from public degree-day data and a term calendar, and marked "modelled".
02

Get the data in three ways

Photograph a bill and let extraction read it, import a spreadsheet with explicit column mapping, or type one meter read at a time. Extracted fields always land in a review screen beside the original document — never straight into your file.

Ambiguous fields — a consumption figure sitting next to a demand charge — raise a written warning, not a quiet lower score.
03

Convert once, at the door

Enter therms, gallons, miles, whatever your invoices use. Everything is normalised to one internal unit set on the way in, so nothing downstream has to guess, and you never see units you don't use.

Your local units are what's shown back to you on every screen and in every export.
04

Simulate, then report the spread

Each factor is sampled across its published interval thousands of times. What you get back is a central figure and a stated confidence range — the same shape of result a reviewer would accept from an environmental consultancy.

Factors are drawn from a published multi-property peer study of school buildings, a national building-energy survey, an internationally recognised value-chain commuting methodology adapted for schools, and a peer-reviewed food-emissions dataset.
“Roughly a third lower than similar schools” is a claim. 1,046 to 1,392 tonnes, transport modelled is a disclosure.

Groundwork is built for the moment after the report is published — the parent email, the board follow-up, the local paper. A stated range holds up under that. A confident single figure with undocumented assumptions behind it does not, and in some jurisdictions it is a liability rather than an embarrassment.

So the software refuses a few things on your behalf: it won't promote a category to a higher tier because you'd like it to, it won't fill a gap with an unlabelled assumption, and it won't show you a percentile against a peer group that doesn't exist yet.

Environmental claims made to the public are regulated in several jurisdictions, including under US federal guidance on environmental marketing and state consumer-protection statutes. Overstated precision is one of the specific failure modes those rules describe. Every Groundwork export carries its method, its period, its tier per category and its range — the things a regulator or a journalist would ask for.
